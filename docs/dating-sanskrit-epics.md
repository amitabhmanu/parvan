## 1. The Problem

Dating an ancient text means answering four separate questions that are constantly
conflated:

1. **When did the events happen** (if any did)?
2. **When did a story about them start circulating orally?**
3. **When was a text composed?**
4. **When did that text reach the form we can actually read?**

For both epics the answers are centuries apart. Almost every popular dispute about
epic chronology is a failure to keep these four apart.

A fifth object matters too, and is usually invisible in debate: the **archetype** —
the common ancestor of all surviving manuscripts, which is not the original.

---

## 2. The Methodological Toolkit

**Language stratigraphy.** Sanskrit changed measurably. Vedic morphology drops out;
epic Sanskrit is post-Vedic but pre-classical, with irregularities Pāṇini's grammar
(c. 350 BCE) does not sanction. Within a single text, layers differ measurably.

**External attestation.** Quotation, citation, or naming by an independently dated
source. The hardest evidence available.

**Internal anachronism.** Peoples, objects, and institutions named in a text cannot
predate their existence. This yields floors.

**Text criticism.** The Poona critical edition of the Mahābhārata (1919–1966) and the
Baroda Rāmāyaṇa (1960–1975) collated manuscripts across Śāradā, Bengali, Devanāgarī,
Malayalam, Telugu and other traditions to reconstruct an archetype. Crucial caveat:
the archetype is the common ancestor of surviving manuscripts, not the original.

**Archaeology.** Material horizons that the text's setting must or must not match.

**Astronomy.** Legitimate in its structural form; unreliable in its
verse-matching form. See §10.

## 3. Evidence from Realia

### The asymmetry principle

**A mentioned thing gives you a floor, not a ceiling.** A passage naming war
elephants is no older than massed elephant warfare — but it may be arbitrarily
younger, because poets archaize deliberately (Homer arms his heroes in bronze during
an iron age).

Since a late item can be interpolated into an old text easily, while an old item
cannot be convincingly inserted into a late one, **floors are reliable per-passage,
and ceilings derived from absence are weak.**

### People

The Mahābhārata's cast has genuine Vedic roots:

- Śantanu appears in the Rigveda
- Parikṣit is praised in the Atharvaveda as a Kuru king
- Janamejaya Pārikṣita performs a horse sacrifice in the Śatapatha and Aitareya
  Brāhmaṇas
- A Dhṛtarāṣṭra Vaicitravīrya appears in the Kāṭhaka Saṃhitā
- Kṛṣṇa Devakīputra studies under Ghora Āṅgirasa in the Chāndogya Upaniṣad

These names circulate in texts of c. 900–600 BCE — but **detached from the epic
plot**. No Vedic source knows a great Kurukṣetra war, a Yudhiṣṭhira, or a Draupadī.

The Rāmāyaṇa has no equivalent. Kosala kings appear in the Śatapatha, but no Rāma
Dāśarathi. Its first external trace is Buddhist: the Pali *Dasaratha Jātaka*, which
has a Rāma-paṇḍita and a Sītā but no Rāvaṇa and no abduction.

Late-dating outsiders in both epics: Yavanas (Greeks), Śakas, Pahlavas, Cīnas (from
Qin, post-3rd c. BCE), Romakas, and Hūṇas (Huns, 4th–5th c. CE).

### Animals

- **Horse.** The domestic lineage behind all modern horses spread out of the
  Pontic-Caspian steppe c. 2000 BCE; spoked-wheel chariots appear at Sintashta about
  then. Horses are essentially absent from Harappan faunal assemblages. Both epics are
  chariot-and-horse narratives throughout. **Neither can depict a world before c. 2000 BCE.**
- **Elephants.** Vedic texts barely use them militarily; the epics deploy massed
  elephant corps in a fourfold army — Magadhan-era warfare of the 6th–4th c. BCE.
- **Southern flora and fauna** in the Rāmāyaṇa's Kiṣkindhākāṇḍa show real Deccan
  familiarity, deepening with Mauryan-era contact.

### Things

- **Iron** (*kṛṣṇa-ayas*) saturates both epics. Iron reaches India c. 1300–1200 BCE,
  abundant in weaponry by c. 800 BCE.
- **Fortified cities** with moats, ramparts, and the *śataghnī* wall engine belong to
  the Second Urbanization.
- **Temples, image worship, *caitya* shrines** are post-Vedic; Vedic practice was
  aniconic.

Three decisive late markers:

| Marker | Floor | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *Eḍūka* (reliquary mound) polemic | post-3rd c. BCE | Anti-Buddhist |
| *Dīnāra* (Roman *denarius*) | post-1st c. CE | In some Rāmāyaṇa passages |
| Greek-derived astrological terms (*horā*, *kendra*, *drekkāṇa*) | post-2nd c. CE | Borrowed into Sanskrit jyotiṣa |

Note: Vyāsa dictating to Gaṇeśa — the epic's famous scene about writing — is absent
from the critical edition entirely.

### Places

- **Mahābhārata core geography** (Kurukṣetra, Hastināpura, Indraprastha, Vāraṇāvata)
  maps onto the Painted Grey Ware distribution. Hastināpura yields PGW occupation
  c. 1100–800 BCE ending in a flood layer, matching a Purāṇic tradition. But PGW sites
  are village-scale, nothing like the epic's jewelled palaces.
- **The Sarasvatī** is a sharp relative marker. The Rigveda describes a mighty river;
  the Mahābhārata describes it vanishing into the sands at Vinaśana. The epic knows the
  post-desiccation state, placing its geographic frame later than Rigvedic times.
- **The Rāmāyaṇa's south** grows vaguer with distance — precise about Mithilā, mythic
  by Kiṣkindhā, wholly fabulous at Laṅkā. That gradient fits hearsay geography of
  c. 500–300 BCE, before Mauryan integration of the peninsula.
- **Ayodhyā** yields no substantial occupation before c. 7th c. BCE.
- **Contested sites.** Offshore work at Dvārakā and Bet Dwarka has produced anchors and
  structures, but securely datable material is largely historical-period. Adam's Bridge
  is a natural shoal-and-sandbar chain formed by sedimentation and sea-level change.

**Negative evidence:** neither epic treats Pāṭaliputra as the imperial centre — an odd
silence if their geographic frames were composed after 300 BCE. One of the better
arguments that the *setting* was fixed earlier than the *text*.

## 4. Stratification

### Six diagnostics

1. **The interruption test.** Excise a passage; if the narrative closes seamlessly —
   often resuming a line it had already spoken (*Wiederaufnahme*, a resumptive seam) —
   the passage is an insertion.
2. **Doctrinal discontinuity.** A hero mortal in one chapter and supreme godhead in
   the next.
3. **Metrical statistics.** Distribution of *triṣṭubh* against *anuṣṭubh*, and
   *vipulā* cadence ratios, which differ measurably between books.
4. **Genre mismatch.** Legal and cosmological discourse inside a battle narrative.
5. **Self-contradicting tables of contents.** The Mahābhārata's own *parvasaṃgraha*
   lists contents that don't match the text; the Spitzer manuscript preserves a third
   list.
6. **Manuscript divergence.** Passages present in southern but not northern recensions.

### Mahābhārata strata

| Layer | Content | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Bardic kernel | Inferred *sūta* praise-poetry on a Kuru succession war | Not recoverable |
| Heroic narrative | Quarrel, dicing, exile, embassy, eighteen days of battle | Kṛṣṇa as shrewd allied chieftain; archaic *triṣṭubh* clusters here |
| Bhārgava redaction | Bhṛgu-clan material (Cyavana, Ruru, Rāma Jāmadagnya) | Sukthankar's thesis: the epic passes from *sūtas* to brahmin custody |
| Theological layer | Bhagavadgītā, Nārāyaṇīya, avatāra doctrine, burden-of-the-Earth framing | Gītā lifts out cleanly; Sañjaya's report resumes exactly |
| Didactic mass | Śānti and Anuśāsana — nearly a fifth of the text | Bhīṣma's death deferred thousands of verses to accommodate them |
| Anthology | Nala, Sāvitrī, Śakuntalā, Rāmopākhyāna, *tīrtha* catalogue | Purāṇic genre wholesale |
| Appendix | Harivaṃśa, labelled *khila* (supplement) | Effectively an early Purāṇa |

**The nested frames** are their own stratigraphy, since each wraps everything inside
it and must be later:

```
Ugraśravas recites to the sages
  └─ Vaiśampāyana recites to Janamejaya
       └─ Sañjaya reports to blind Dhṛtarāṣṭra
            └─ The eighteen days at Kurukṣetra
                 └─ Kṛṣṇa speaks to Arjuna
```

The Gītā reaches us as a god's speech, reported by a clairvoyant charioteer, to a
blind king, recited by a disciple at a snake sacrifice, retold by a bard to forest
ascetics. Every layer of that chain is a compositional decision by someone later.

### Rāmāyaṇa strata

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|
| Core (books 2–6) | Ayodhyā through Yuddha. Rāma mortal. Homogeneous polished Sanskrit — the *ādikāvya* |
| Seams inside the core | Yuddhakāṇḍa's divine-assembly ending (a retrofit); Ādityahṛdaya hymn; Yavana/Śaka lists |
| Bāla and Uttara | Avatāra theology; Viśvāmitra childhood; Ahalyā; Rāvaṇa's back-story; Śambūka; Sītā's banishment; Lava-Kuśa reciting the poem to Rāma |
| Later accretion | Regional expansion in the manuscript traditions |

Brockington's scheme: core c. 5th c. BCE; major expansion 4th c. BCE – 1st c. CE;
Bāla and Uttara by 3rd c. CE; minor additions thereafter.

### The shared pattern

- Mortal hero → incarnate deity
- Taut plot → encyclopedic compendium
- *Sūta* bardic voice → brahmin redactorial voice
- Single telling → nested frames that authorize the telling
- Regional geography → subcontinental horizon
- Sacrificial religion → bhakti and temple religion

Two markers appear in both and are always late: ***phalaśruti*** verses promising
merit to hearers (they mark the boundaries of whatever counted as a complete unit
when written), and **Kali Yuga prophecies**, the outermost skin of both texts.

### Caveats

Late insertion does not imply late material — the Nala story is probably an ancient
folk tale that entered the Mahābhārata late. Scholars cut differently: Holtzmann
argued for a wholesale ideological inversion in which the Kauravas were originally
the heroes (now largely rejected, never fully refuted). And everything below the
archetype is inference from internal evidence, not manuscript witness.

## 5. Language and Metre

**The key finding: the epics' Sanskrit is not archaic.** It is post-Vedic,
non-Pāṇinian, and drifting toward Middle Indo-Aryan.

Traditional grammarians labelled deviant epic forms *ārṣa* — "of the seers" — meaning
licensed irregularity. That label is itself evidence: by the time commentators worked
on these texts, the epic language already looked wrong.

### Features

**Morphology.** Vedic subjunctive and injunctive gone. A dozen Vedic infinitive
formations collapse into *-tum*. Pitch accent entirely absent. The three past tenses
lose aspectual distinction and become interchangeable narrative pasts. Active/middle
voice contrast breaks down, selected for metrical convenience in ways Pāṇini forbids.

**Syntax.** Long chains of absolutives in *-tvā* replace finite subordination. Past
passive participles as main predicates — the construction that becomes the ergative
past across Indo-Aryan. Paratactic sentences padded with formulaic vocatives
(*bharatarṣabha*, *mahābāho*, *viśāṃ pate*) — the fingerprint of oral-formulaic
composition.

**Lexicon.** Prakritic intrusions and hypersanskritisms — words wrongly "corrected"
back into Sanskrit, which only happens when the corrector thinks in a Middle Indic
vernacular.

### The Pāṇini test

If the epics predated Pāṇini, their deviations should be *archaisms* matching Vedic.
They mostly aren't. They are simplifications running in the direction Middle
Indo-Aryan was already moving. **The epics don't preserve older grammar; they
anticipate later grammar.** This is a strong argument against extreme antiquity,
independent of archaeology or astronomy.

Genuine archaisms survive, concentrated in *triṣṭubh* passages — so the language is
stratified too.

### Metre

The śloka has four *pāda*s of eight syllables. Positions 5–7 carry the cadence:

```
Odd pāda  (1st, 3rd):   . . . .  ˘ – –  .
Even pāda (2nd, 4th):   . . . .  ˘ – ˘  .
                        1 2 3 4  5 6 7  8
```

That standard form is the *pathyā*. Odd pādas may substitute other patterns at 5–7 —
the *vipulā*s, of which four types are common. Two things change measurably over time:

1. The proportion of vipulās to pathyās declines as the metre regularizes.
2. The relative frequency ranking of the four vipulā types shifts.

Hopkins first exploited this; Brockington used vipulā ratios to separate the
Rāmāyaṇa's books into strata. The Vedic anuṣṭubh had no such fixed cadence, so the
śloka's development is itself a chronological arrow, and both epics sit at an
intermediate point on it.

**Compound length** is a second metric. Epic compounds run to a few members; mature
classical *kāvya* (Bāṇa, Daṇḍin, 6th–7th c.) piles up ten or fifteen. The epics never
do this — placing their core before high classical style.

**Elaborate classical metres** (*vasantatilakā*, *mandākrāntā*, *śārdūlavikrīḍita*)
are almost absent from the core. Aśvaghoṣa uses them fluently by the 1st–2nd c. CE,
so their scarcity is meaningful.

### Limits

Epic Sanskrit was probably nobody's speech — like Homeric Greek, a *Kunstsprache*
blending forms from different centuries and regions. Formulaic diction preserves
obsolete forms indefinitely, so archaisms date the *tradition*, not the composition.

And scribes normalized toward the Pāṇinian standard, so the surviving text is
grammatically tidier than what was composed. The linguistic evidence gives a
*minimum* level of irregularity; the originals were probably rougher and more
Prakrit-inflected — which pushes the estimate later, not earlier.

## 6. Physical Materials and Transmission

### Nothing old survives

There is no ancient manuscript of either epic. The earliest substantially complete
witnesses are second-millennium CE copies. A few Nepalese palm-leaf codices push
further back — the Baroda editors used a Rāmāyaṇa manuscript of c. 1020 CE. Against a
composition window of roughly 400 BCE – 400 CE, that leaves a gap of six centuries at
minimum and more like a millennium and a half for the earliest layers.

### Why

| Medium | Region | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Birch bark (*bhūrjapatra*) | North | Delaminates, becomes brittle; survives only dry and cold |
| Palm leaf (*tālapatra*) | South and east | 3–6 centuries in tropical humidity before recopying or loss |
| Paper | North, from c. 14th c. | Why so many northern copies cluster there |

Against these: monsoon humidity, termites, silverfish, fungal growth. No Egyptian
desert, no Herculaneum ash, no Dead Sea cave.

The consequence is almost comic: **our oldest physical trace of the Mahābhārata was
found in Chinese Turkestan.** The Spitzer manuscript — palm-leaf fragments from Kizil,
2nd–4th c. CE — survived because the Taklamakan is dry. The Gilgit birch-bark
manuscripts survived in high-altitude cold. The physical record of Indian literature
exists mainly where India's climate doesn't.

### What recopying does

Every surviving manuscript sits twenty to forty copying generations from the
archetype. Scribes correct against other exemplars — *contamination* — and Sukthankar
found the Mahābhārata tradition so contaminated that no clean stemma could be built.
The Poona edition is a reasoned reconstruction, not a mechanical descent from a tree.

Script change leaves useful residue. Brāhmī diversified into Śāradā, Nāgarī, Bengali,
Newari, Grantha, Telugu, Malayalam. Characters that look alike in one script don't in
another, so a variant explicable as a Śāradā misreading tells you that reading passed
through Kashmir.

### The oral question

The epics lacked what the Veda had. Vedic transmission used the *padapāṭha*, then
*krama*, *jaṭā*, and *ghana* recitations permuting words in fixed patterns — an
error-detecting code that preserved the Rigveda phoneme-for-phoneme across millennia.

**The epics had none of it.** They were sung by *sūtas* and later *kathakas*, with
expansion and improvisation as normal practice. Fluidity was a feature.

This single contrast explains why the Rigveda transmits with astonishing fidelity
while the Mahābhārata grows from a claimed 8,800 verses to 100,000. The search for an
"original manuscript" is looking for something the mode of composition and the climate
together guarantee never existed in recoverable form.

### What carries the load instead

- **Copper plates and stone.** Epigraphy does the work manuscripts cannot.
- **Sculpture.** Rāmāyaṇa panels at Deogarh, terracottas at Nagarjunakonda and
  Paharpur, Pallava and Chalukyan reliefs. These date the *reception* of specific
  episodes independently of any text.

## 7. External Attestation

### Before the common era

| Source | Date | What it attests | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Āśvalāyana Gṛhyasūtra lists "Bhārata" and "Mahābhārata" | 4th–3rd c. BCE | A work by that name | Sūtra's own date disputed; ritual lists are interpolatable |
| Pāṇini on devotees of Vāsudeva and Arjuna | c. 350 BCE | The cult and the pairing | Does not attest a text |
| Megasthenes (via Arrian, Strabo) on "Herakles" among the Śūrasenas | c. 300 BCE | Kṛṣṇa-Vāsudeva worship at Mathurā | *Interpretatio graeca* |
| Heliodorus pillar, Besnagar | c. 110 BCE | Vāsudeva as "god of gods"; wording parallels a Mahābhārata verse | Quotation or shared commonplace? |
| Nānāghāṭ, Ghosundi inscriptions | 1st c. BCE | Saṃkarṣaṇa and Vāsudeva as a divine pair | — |
| Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* on staged Kaṃsa performances | c. 150 BCE | Kṛṣṇa-cycle material publicly performed | — |
| Arthaśāstra citing Duryodhana | contested | — | Text's own date too contested to anchor |
| Pali Jātakas (*Dasaratha*, *Ghata*) | verses 3rd–2nd c. BCE | Rāma and Vṛṣṇi material | Surrounding prose is 5th c. CE commentary |

### First to fifth centuries CE

| Source | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Aśvaghoṣa naming Vālmīki as a metrical innovator | 1st–2nd c. CE | Earliest independent attestation of Vālmīki as a named *author* |
| Dio Chrysostom on Indians singing Homer | c. 100 CE | Suggestive only — could be actual Homer via Indo-Greek contact |
| **Spitzer manuscript**, Kizil | c. 200–300 CE | Earliest physical trace; *parvan* list differs from ours |
| Bhāsa's plays (*Ūrubhaṅga*, *Karṇabhāra*, *Pratimānāṭaka*, etc.) | 2nd–4th c. CE | Alters outcomes — presupposes a known received version. Date and attribution contested |
| Vimalasūri's *Paumacariyaṃ* (Prakrit Jain Rāmāyaṇa) | 1st–5th c. CE | Argues *against* Vālmīki — you only polemicize against an authority |
| Kālidāsa's *Raghuvaṃśa*, *Abhijñānaśākuntala* | 4th–5th c. CE | Reworks both epics substantially |

### Fifth century onward: the decisive epigraphy

- **Gupta and post-Gupta copper-plate grants.** The Khoh plates of Sarvanātha (early
  6th c.) cite the epic as the hundred-thousand-verse collection compiled by Vyāsa.
  More broadly, the standard imprecatory verses warning donors against revoking land
  grants are quoted from the Mahābhārata in **hundreds of grants from the 5th century
  CE forward**. By then the text is fixed, sized, attributed, and legally citable.
- **Veal Kantel inscription (Cambodia, 6th c.)** endows daily recitation of the
  Rāmāyaṇa, the Purāṇas, and the complete Bhārata.
- **Aihole inscription of Pulakeśin II (634 CE)** dates itself 3,735 years after the
  Bhārata war — dating the *tradition* of the 3102 BCE reckoning, not the war.

### Two structural limits

1. Almost every witness attests **stories and characters** rather than **our text**.
2. A first surviving reference is not a first reference. The Indian archive is so
   lossy that the true earliest citation was almost certainly written on something
   that rotted.

## 8. Cross-Cultural Diffusion

Epic ideas travelled enormously — just not westward.

### Eastward: overwhelming

| Waypoint | Date |
|---|---|
| *Liudu jijing*, Rāma narrative in Chinese (tr. Kang Senghui) | 251 CE |
| Veal Kantel, Cambodia: complete Bhārata endowed | c. 600 CE |
| Cham inscription, temple dedicated to Vālmīki | 7th c. CE |
| Old Javanese *Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa* (from Bhaṭṭi, not Vālmīki directly) | c. 870 CE |
| Old Javanese *Bhāratayuddha* | 1157 CE |
| Angkor Wat Kurukṣetra and Laṅkā reliefs | c. 1150 CE |

Then full localization: Thai *Ramakien*, Khmer *Reamker*, Lao *Phra Lak Phra Lam*,
Burmese *Yama Zatdaw*, Malay *Hikayat Seri Rama*, Maranao *Maharadia Lawana*.
Northward: Tibetan and Khotanese Rāmāyaṇas among the Dunhuang manuscripts.

### Westward: sparse, and mostly other texts

Contact was never the problem — Achaemenid administration, Alexander, Seleucids,
Indo-Greeks, Red Sea trade in the *Periplus*. And Indian material *did* move west:
the *Pañcatantra* into Middle Persian, then Syriac, then Arabic as *Kalīla
wa-Dimna*, then Greek, Hebrew, Latin. The Buddha's biography became Barlaam and
Josaphat ("Josaphat" < *bodhisattva*). Suśruta's medicine, the *Siddhānta*
astronomy, decimal numerals, chess.

Why not the epics?

1. **Portability.** The *Pañcatantra* is detachable fables with transferable morals.
   Kurukṣetra cannot be lifted out of the Kuru genealogy, the dharma framework, and a
   sacred geography meaningless outside India.
2. **Who was carrying.** Westward transmission ran through Buddhist monks, merchants,
   and later Persian scholars. Buddhists carried Rāma material *in Jātaka form* — which
   is exactly the shape it takes in Chinese in 251 CE. The channel determined the content.
3. **What receivers wanted.** Southeast Asian rulers were adopting Sanskritic kingship
   as legitimation, so they imported the whole package. Persia and Rome wanted goods,
   medicine, and mathematics.
4. **It may be there, disguised.** *Interpretatio graeca* flattened foreign gods into
   Greek ones. Any epic content reaching Greek ears was translated into Greek myth on
   arrival. Absence in the Greek record is partly an artefact of how Greeks wrote.

When a scholarly tradition finally engaged, it got there immediately: **al-Bīrūnī
(c. 1030)** describes the eighteen *parvans* and quotes the Gītā at length. Akbar's
**Razmnāma** (1580s) is a full Persian Mahābhārata.

### Dating payoff

Hard *termini ante quem*, independent of Indian manuscript transmission: a Rāma
narrative in Chinese by 251 CE; both epics complete and liturgically endowed in
Cambodia by c. 600 CE; Southeast Asian versions incorporating Uttarakāṇḍa material,
confirming the late books were attached before export.

The Chinese version is lean — no Rāvaṇa abduction — like the Pali Jātaka. Genuinely
ambiguous: either Buddhist redactors stripped it, or a simpler version still
circulated in the 3rd century.

### The deep comparative layer

Wikander (1947) and Dumézil mapped the Pāṇḍava brothers onto reconstructed
Indo-European social functions. If sound, this dates an *inherited narrative template*
to the shared ancestor tradition. Handle with care — the framework has been criticized
for finding trifunctional patterns everywhere, and it says nothing about when any
Sanskrit text was composed.

## 9. Evolving Ideas as Evidence

A whole worldview is much harder to interpolate than a word. A scribe can insert
*dīnāra* into a verse; he cannot retrofit a conceptual framework into a narrative
built without it.

### Religion

- **Sacrifice recedes, devotion arrives.** *Bhakti* is not Vedic; it appears in embryo
  in the Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad (c. 400–200 BCE). The Gītā presupposes it fully formed.
- **Avatāra doctrine is visibly under construction.** The Mahābhārata's own lists of
  Viṣṇu's descents are inconsistent with each other. A settled doctrine produces stable
  lists; an emerging one produces exactly this instability. The epic is one of the
  places where the doctrine got made.
- **Temple and image worship** assumed throughout; archaeology puts those from c. 2nd
  c. BCE. **Pilgrimage** likewise post-Vedic — the Vanaparvan's *tīrtha* catalogue
  presupposes an established network with an economy behind it.
- **Ahiṃsā and vegetarianism** are stratified: unembarrassed kṣatriya meat-eating
  alongside passages praising abstention, the latter responding to the Buddhist and
  Jain challenge that Aśoka made state ideology.

### Philosophy

- **Proto-Sāṃkhya.** The Gītā and Mokṣadharma use Sāṃkhya-Yoga vocabulary constantly
  (*puruṣa*, *prakṛti*, the *guṇas*) but **not** the fixed twenty-five-*tattva* scheme
  of Īśvarakṛṣṇa's *Sāṃkhyakārikā* (c. 350–450 CE). Had they been composed after
  classical Sāṃkhya was codified, they would have used its settled terminology.
  **This caps the didactic material at roughly the 4th century CE**, independently of
  manuscripts, inscriptions, or realia.
- Negatively: no six-*darśana* scheme, no organized Vedānta, no Brahmasūtra
  presupposition.
- **Karma, rebirth, mokṣa** are assumed everywhere — none Rigvedic, all emerging in
  the Bṛhadāraṇyaka and Chāndogya c. 700–500 BCE. Firm floor. And the Mahābhārata
  *argues about* whether mokṣa belongs alongside dharma, artha, kāma — the four-goal
  scheme is being negotiated in the text, not inherited by it.
- **Materialism as a named opponent.** Jābāli's Lokāyata speech in the Rāmāyaṇa;
  Cārvāka's hostile cameo in the Mahābhārata. You cannot refute a school that doesn't
  exist.
- **The yuga system** has no Vedic ancestry; its arithmetic (432,000 years and
  multiples) fits post-Hellenistic great-year speculation. Outermost layer in both.

### Political and social

- **Kingship scales up.** The Vedic *rājan* leads a tribe; the epics assume a
  *cakravartin* horizon, *digvijaya*, tribute from named distant peoples — the
  Nanda-Mauryan imagination.
- **Political theory appears.** The Śāntiparvan's *daṇḍanīti*, and a state-of-nature
  argument in which the absence of royal coercion means *matsya-nyāya*, the law of
  fishes.
- **Caste hardens.** Movement toward birth-fixed hierarchy with acute anxiety about
  *varṇasaṃkara*. The Śambūka episode reflects an orthodoxy harder than anything in
  the Rāmāyaṇa's core.

### The best instrument: institutional embarrassment

The Mahābhārata's entire genealogy depends on *niyoga*, levirate union. Vyāsa fathers
Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Pāṇḍu on his brother's widows; Kuntī's sons are begotten by gods under
the same principle. **The plot cannot exist without it.** Yet later Dharmaśāstra
prohibits the practice, and the epic's own later layers explain that niyoga was
permissible in former ages but is not now.

Draupadī's polyandry works the same way — justified via a misheard command, a
prior-life boon, appeals to precedent, because by the time of redaction it had become
scandalous.

**This proves what no other method can: a real temporal gap between the formation of
the story and the sensibility of the people transmitting it.** Redactors do not invent
embarrassments. They inherit them and apologize. The apology is dateable even when the
story isn't.

### Where this method is weakest

- **Circularity.** Manu, the Arthaśāstra, and several Upaniṣads are themselves dated
  partly against the epics. The honest procedure anchors on externally dated things —
  Aśokan edicts, the Heliodorus pillar, datable Buddhist texts — and treats
  epic-to-Dharmaśāstra comparison as relative.
- **Staged debate is not endorsement.** These texts voice positions in order to defeat
  them.
- **Conservatism cuts both ways.** Obsolete institutions survive as narrative
  furniture — which is exactly what makes the embarrassment signal valuable, since it
  distinguishes furniture the redactors were comfortable with from furniture they had
  to explain away.

## 10. Further Channels

### Iconography and numismatics

The most underrated line, because it dates *reception* independently of any text.

**The most precise single item:** silver coins of the Indo-Greek king Agathocles,
excavated at Ai-Khanoum, c. 180 BCE, depicting Vāsudeva-Kṛṣṇa with wheel and conch and
Saṃkarṣaṇa-Balarāma with plough and club. Two brothers, correct attributes, dated state
issue from a Greek mint.

Then: terracottas and panels at Nagarjunakonda and Pawaya (2nd–4th c. CE); Gupta
Rāmāyaṇa and Kṛṣṇa panels at Deogarh; Pallava and early Chalukyan reliefs.

The technique's real power is **negative**: episodes absent from the early visual
record and appearing only later — much Uttarakāṇḍa material — corroborate their
textual lateness. Two independent media agreeing on a stratification is far more
persuasive than either alone.

### Genealogical reckoning

The Purāṇas state an interval between Parikṣit and Mahāpadma Nanda — 1,015 years in
some recensions, 1,050 or 1,500 in others. Since Nanda is fixed near 350 BCE by Greek
sources, this yields a war date around 1400–1500 BCE. Pargiter built a chronology on
this.

Problems: manuscripts disagree; oral king lists expand systematically (inserting
ancestors is prestigious, deleting them is not); lists contain demonstrable
duplications and telescopings. The output is an order of magnitude — but it lands far
closer to the archaeological horizon than any astronomical claim.

### Astronomy, properly

**The legitimate version.** The useful signal is the *structure of the nakṣatra
system*, not planetary configurations. Vedic lists head the lunar mansions with
Kṛttikā; later lists with Aśvinī. That shift tracks precession and is a genuine
relative marker — and the Mahābhārata contains passages reflecting **both**
conventions, itself evidence of layering. Also: solstitial colure references, and the
Vanaparvan's remark about Abhijit slipping from the sky.

**The illegitimate version.** Verse-matching against ephemeris software. Results
include 5561 BCE (Vartak, Oak), 3067 BCE (Achar), ~1478 BCE (Iyengar) for the war;
12209 BCE, 7323 BCE, 5114 BCE for Rāma's birth. That spread — over seven millennia
from the same corpus — is the diagnostic. Failure modes:

- **Underdetermination.** No single date satisfies all astronomical verses; every
  researcher discards a subset, chosen after the target date is in view.
- **Cheap matches.** Precession makes configurations recur. Search 10,000 years with
  loose tolerances across thousands of candidate verses and hits are combinatorially
  guaranteed.
- **Genre error.** "Two eclipses in thirteen days," Mars retrograde near Jyeṣṭhā — a
  stock omen repertoire signalling doom, not a log.
- **Interpolation.** Later astronomers had motive and access.
- **Material impossibility.** A 5561 BCE Kurukṣetra with iron weapons and horse-drawn
  chariots isn't a chronology dispute, it's a category error.

**On 3102 BCE.** The Kali Yuga epoch was *back-computed* by astronomers around
Āryabhaṭa's time from an assumed grand conjunction. That is a calculation propagating
forward, not a memory reaching back.

**A fair concession:** 19th-century European dating was often arbitrary — Max Müller
admitted his Vedic chronology rested on guesswork. The modern consensus doesn't rest
on him; it rests on convergence.

### Computational methods

Stylometry (function-word frequencies, n-gram distributions, morphological ratios)
applied to the critical editions broadly recovers the strata that traditional
philology identified by hand — a meaningful independent check. The Gītā separates from
the surrounding Bhīṣmaparvan on purely statistical grounds.

Phylogenetic software handles the Rāmāyaṇa reasonably but struggles with the
Mahābhārata, for the reason Sukthankar identified: contamination between branches
violates the tree model. Horizontal transfer is what these algorithms handle worst.

These refine *relative* stratification. They cannot produce absolute dates — no
external calibration.

### Physical analysis

Palaeography dates manuscripts by letter forms; radiocarbon dates the substrate
directly; ink composition and codicology add resolution. None touches composition, but
it firms up transmission history.

unknown antiquity and the whole floor softens.

## 13. A Generalized Procedure

### The core insight

Stratifying first is necessary, not merely convenient. Dating an unstratified
composite text produces a meaningless number — you get the date of whichever layer you
sampled. The Mahābhārata "dates" to 400 BCE or 400 CE depending on which passage you
interrogate, and neither answer is wrong. Only after you know there are layers does
asking "when?" become well-formed.

### Three complications

**The steps interact rather than running in sequence.** Stratification hypotheses are
tested by whether they yield coherent date ranges. If a proposed layer contains both a
Hūṇa reference and a genuinely archaic *triṣṭubh* passage, you cut it wrong. Dating
results feed back and revise the stratification. This is the hermeneutic circle —
productive rather than vicious as long as the constraints are independent.

**Floors and ceilings aren't symmetric.** Floors come mostly from intrinsic content and
are strong. Ceilings come mostly from extrinsic attestation and are weak, because the
archive is lossy: *first surviving reference is not first reference*. Any
confident-looking bracket should be read as tighter on its left edge than its right.

There is also a category error worth naming: a floor from an intrinsic feature bounds
*that passage*; a ceiling from an external citation bounds *the thing cited*, which may
be an earlier form of the work. They aren't bounding the same object, so they don't
straightforwardly compose into an interval.

**"The text" isn't one object.** There are at least four: the depicted world, the oral
tradition, the composed text, and the surviving archetype. Each stratum has all four,
with different dates. The Mahābhārata's war narrative depicts a world of roughly 900
BCE, was composed around 300 BCE, and survives in a Gupta-period archetype. All three
are correct answers to different questions.

### The procedure

0. **Establish the archetype** through recension and critical editing. Otherwise you
   assign dates to variants that entered in the twelfth century.
1. **Stratify by intrinsic discontinuity** — doctrinal, linguistic, metrical,
   structural, narrative.
2. **Set floors per stratum**, mostly intrinsic: realia, borrowings, presupposed
   institutions and concepts.
3. **Set ceilings per stratum**, mostly extrinsic: citation, physical witness,
   iconographic reception, translation.
4. **Iterate 1–3** until layers and dates are mutually consistent.
5. **Score each argument for independence and defeasibility**, then look for
   convergence. A bracket supported by six correlated arguments is worth much less than
   one supported by three independent ones.
6. **Distrust arguments from silence explicitly** — score them separately, since a lossy
   archive generates false silences constantly.
7. **State separately what is being dated** — world, tradition, composition, or
   archetype.

### Scope

This works well beyond Sanskrit. It is recognizably how the Documentary Hypothesis
approaches the Pentateuch, how synoptic scholarship handles the Gospels, and how
Homeric analysts work on the Iliad. The specific diagnostics vary by tradition; the
architecture doesn't.

It strains on texts with a *single* author and short composition period. There
stratification returns nothing, and you fall back entirely on extrinsic evidence —
which is why a lost minor Greek tragedy can be harder to date than the Mahābhārata
despite being a hundredth the length. **Layering is a nuisance for the reader and a
gift to the dater.**

## 14. The Network Formalization

### The formal object

A constraint satisfaction problem over a graph. Each node is a **stratum** with an
interval `[f_i, c_i]`. Edges are ordering relations, in three flavors:

| Edge type | Constraint | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Citation** — A quotes or presupposes B | `f_A ≥ f_B` | Directed; direction is often the hard part |
| **Containment** — A frames B | A is later | Directed and reliable (e.g. the Uttarakāṇḍa's swallowing frame) |
| **Shared realia / concept** — A and B both presuppose institution first attested at *t* | both floors → *t* | Undirected; more a shared latent variable than an edge |

Propagation:

```
f_i ← max(f_i, max over predecessors j→i of f_j)
c_i ← min(c_i, min over successors i→k of c_k)
```

Iterate to fixpoint.

This is **arc consistency on interval constraints**. If the graph is acyclic and all
constraints monotone, iteration converges monotonically and the fixpoint is unique
regardless of update order — intervals only shrink and are bounded below, so
termination is guaranteed. Genuinely useful property: *the answer doesn't depend on
which scholar starts where.*

### Four ways it gets interesting

**1. Cycles exist and are informative.** The circularity noted in §9 — Manu dated
partly against the epics, the epics dated partly against Manu — is a cycle. Under pure
interval propagation, a cycle with a strict ordering relation is unsatisfiable and the
intervals empty out. Which is diagnostically useful: an infeasible cycle tells you a
dependency direction is wrong, or that two strata have been conflated into one node.
**Detecting inconsistency is doing work.**

**2. Anchors carry the whole system.** A pure propagation network with no externally
fixed nodes has a huge solution space — everything shifts together. The system is
pinned only by nodes dated by **non-textual** means: the Agathocles coins, Aśokan
edicts, radiocarbon, Chinese translation records with dynastic dates. These are the
boundary conditions.

This reframes the enterprise: **textual dating produces a rigid *structure*;
archaeology and epigraphy supply the few points that fix it in absolute time.**

And it makes a checkable prediction: *the further a stratum sits, in graph distance,
from the nearest external anchor, the wider its interval should be.* Which is what we
observe — the Mahābhārata's late layers sit adjacent to copper plates and are tight;
the hypothetical bardic kernel is many hops from anything and spans centuries.

**3. Edges are uncertain, not just nodes.** Whether the Rāmopākhyāna depends on
Vālmīki; whether the *Dasaratha Jātaka* is ancestor or abridgement — these are edges of
contested direction. Propagating them as certain overstates precision badly.

Here interval propagation is the wrong tool and **Bayesian inference** is right: put a
probability on each edge's direction, priors on node dates, and sample. You get
posterior distributions rather than intervals, and you can ask **which uncertain edge,
if resolved, would tighten the network most**. That quantity — expected information
gain per edge — would be genuinely useful, because it tells you where scholarly effort
pays.

**4. Equilibrium isn't truth.** The fixpoint is the tightest assignment consistent with
your constraints. If a constraint is wrong, the system converges *confidently* to a
wrong answer, and the convergence provides no warning. This is precisely the failure
mode of astronomical dating: internally consistent within its own subgraph,
disconnected from the material anchors that would falsify it. **Any real implementation
needs conflict detection *between* subgraphs, not just propagation within them.**

### What this reframes

The convergence argument gets a precise statement: independent methods agreeing means
**multiple disjoint paths through the graph, from different anchors, arriving at
overlapping intervals for the same node**. Under Bayesian treatment, path-disjointness
is exactly what makes evidence multiply rather than merely repeat. Correlated arguments
share edges and add much less than their count suggests.

It also explains why single dramatic claims can't move the consensus. Overturning a
well-connected node requires either breaking an anchor or falsifying many edges
simultaneously, each needing independent motivation. **High graph connectivity is what
makes an interval robust** — and equally, what makes a poorly-connected node like the
bardic kernel resistant to ever being pinned down.

### Status

As far as I know, nobody has built this. Phylogenetic methods have been applied *within*
single manuscript traditions, but a cross-textual dating network over Sanskrit strata,
with explicit anchors and edge uncertainties, does not exist. The inputs are largely
available in the critical editions and epigraphic corpora.

## Appendix: The Bottom Line

| | Mahābhārata | Rāmāyaṇa |
|---|---|---|
| Possible historical kernel | c. 900 BCE (PGW horizon, Vedic-attested Kuru kings) | No comparable Vedic footprint |
| Narrative core composed | c. 400–100 BCE | c. 500–200 BCE |
| Major expansion | through c. 400 CE | Bāla & Uttara by c. 300 CE |
| Fixed and citable | c. 450–500 CE | c. 600 CE |
| Earliest physical witness | Spitzer ms, c. 250 CE (parvan list differs) | None early; archetype is Gupta-period |
| Strength profile | Better attested from outside | Better dissected from inside |

**The central inversion:** older story, younger text (Mahābhārata); younger story,
older text (Rāmāyaṇa). And if either has a historical kernel, Kuru dominance
(c. 1000–800 BCE) precedes Kosala's rise (c. 700–500 BCE) — meaning the *setting* order
may also invert tradition's Tretā-before-Dvāpara sequence.

**Why the consensus holds:** no mechanism explains why scribal normalization, Greek
coin dies, Sāṃkhya's degree of systematization, and Cambodian temple endowments would
all conspire to produce the same bracket if the bracket were wrong.
